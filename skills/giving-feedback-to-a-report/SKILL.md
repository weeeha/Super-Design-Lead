# Giving Feedback to a Report

## Overview

The manager-side of design crit: how to deliver feedback to a person who reports to you, in a way that lands without crushing motivation and serves their growth.

This is different from `SuperDesign:critiquing`. That skill is about the *work*. This skill is about the *delivery* — how feedback travels from your head into their development.

**Delayed feedback compounds.** Every week you defer hard feedback, the gap between expectation and behavior grows, and the conversation gets harder.

## The Iron Law

```
NO FEEDBACK WITHOUT THE THREE FRAMES
```

Every piece of feedback to a direct report goes through three frames before delivery:

1. **Observation** — what specifically happened (behavior, artifact, moment). Not interpretation.
2. **Impact** — what effect it had (on the work, the team, the user, on you). Concrete.
3. **Ask** — what you'd want to see differently going forward. Specific, testable.

If you can't fill all three frames, the feedback isn't ready. Hold it; don't give it half-baked.

**No exceptions for:**
- "Quick informal feedback"
- "Just a heads-up"
- "Small thing"
- "I've already mentioned it once" (especially this — repeat feedback needs MORE structure, not less)

## The Process

### 1. Identify what you're really giving

Three flavors of feedback, each with different delivery:

| Flavor | When | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| **Reinforcing** | Specific behavior you want to see continue | Concrete, timely, public-OK |
| **Developmental** | Gap from current level or trajectory | Private, growth-oriented, "I want to help you get there" |
| **Corrective** | Behavior that crossed a line or risks a relationship/career | Private, direct, action-oriented |

Misclassifying a corrective issue as developmental is how reports get blindsided at performance review. Be honest about the flavor.

### 2. Build the three frames

```
**Observation:** On <date>, in <context>, I noticed <specific behavior or artifact>.
                 (No "always", "never", "you tend to" — those are interpretations, not observations.)

**Impact:** This affected <work / team / user / you> in <specific way>.
            (Why does this matter? Be concrete.)

**Ask:** Going forward, I'd want to see <specific behavior or outcome>.
         (What would "fixed" look like? How will we both know?)
```

### 3. Apply the SBI/SBI-I check (Situation-Behavior-Impact)

For each frame, ask:
- **Situation:** is the context concrete enough that they know exactly which moment you mean?
- **Behavior:** is it observable (a video could capture it), or are you describing an interpretation?
- **Impact:** is the consequence concrete, or vague ("it didn't feel right")?

If any are vague, sharpen them.

### 4. Anticipate their first response

The skill of feedback isn't just delivering it — it's hearing their response without flinching.

Predict what they might say:
- **"That's not what happened"** → return to specifics. Don't argue interpretation; return to observation.
- **"I disagree"** → ask what they observed instead. Sometimes you were wrong. Often you weren't but they need to be heard.
- **"I'm overwhelmed"** → acknowledge, but don't drop the feedback. "I hear you. We still need to address this — let's discuss what support looks like."
- **Tears / shutdown** → stop. Acknowledge. Reschedule the rest of the conversation if needed. Don't pile on.
- **"I'll do better"** without specifics → that's not closure. Ask: "What specifically will you change?"

### 5. Plan the follow-up

Feedback without follow-up is venting. Plan now:
- When will you check in on this?
- What specific evidence will tell you the ask is being met?
- What's the consequence if it isn't?

Put the follow-up in the report's open threads.

### 6. Deliver

Setting:
- **Private** for developmental and corrective. **Never in front of others** for these.
- **Soon after the event** — within a week, ideally within days. Older context = harder conversation.
- **Don't bundle** — one feedback per delivery. Two compounds; three is an ambush.

Opening: don't soften with throat-clearing. "I want to give you some feedback" is better than "How are you doing? Oh great, by the way..."

Pace: deliver the three frames, then pause. Let them respond. Don't fill silence.

Close: align on the ask and follow-up. Capture both.

## Output Template

```
## Feedback Prep — <report name> — <date>

### Flavor
[ ] Reinforcing  [ ] Developmental  [ ] Corrective

### Three frames
**Observation:** ...

**Impact:** ...

**Ask:** ...

### Anticipated response + handling
- If they say "X" → respond with Y
- If they say "Z" → respond with W

### Follow-up
- When I'll check: <date>
- What I'll look for: <specific evidence>
- Consequence if ask isn't met: <if corrective>

### Setting
- Time / place: <specific>
- Channel: in-person / video / async (avoid Slack for corrective)
```

## Common Rationalizations

| Excuse | Reality |
|--------|---------|
| "I'll just mention it casually next 1:1" | Casual mentions don't land. Use the three frames. |
| "I don't want to make a big deal of it" | If it's worth giving, it's worth giving well. |
| "They'll figure it out" | They won't. They'll keep doing it. |
| "I've already mentioned it" | Mentioning ≠ delivering structured feedback. Try again, properly. |
| "I'll save it for perf review" | Perf review feedback should never be a surprise. Give it now. |
| "It might hurt their feelings" | Withholding hurts more — they'll be blindsided later. |
| "It's actually two things" | Then you have two pieces of feedback to deliver, separately. |
| "Hard feedback over Slack is fine" | It isn't. Almost ever. |

## Red Flags — STOP

- Bundling 2+ pieces of feedback in one delivery
- Delivering corrective feedback in public or in front of peers
- Delivering hard feedback over Slack/email
- Skipping the "ask" because "they should know what to do"
- Skipping follow-up planning
- Repeating feedback without a structured retry
- Delivering when you're angry (wait 24 hours minimum)

**All mean: stop. Restructure or reschedule.**
