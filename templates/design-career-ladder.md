# Design Career Ladder

> **Customize for your org.** This is a starter template — the levels, names, and expectations should match your company's framework. Adjust language, level names (IC1 vs L3 vs Designer I), and weights to fit reality.

## Levels at a glance

| Level | Years | Scope | Autonomy | Influence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IC1 — Designer** | 0-2 | Feature within a defined project | Direction from senior | Within their immediate team |
| **IC2 — Designer II** | 2-4 | Full feature, with input on framing | Defines own daily work | Within team |
| **IC3 — Senior Designer** | 4-7 | Full project end-to-end | Owns project decisions | Across adjacent teams |
| **IC4 — Staff Designer** | 7+ | Multi-project or product area | Sets approach, partners with PM/Eng leadership | Across the org |
| **IC5 — Principal Designer** | 10+ | Product line or org-wide capability | Sets strategy | Beyond the immediate org |

## Capability dimensions

Score each level on these dimensions. Lower levels = developing. Higher levels = exemplary.

### 1. Craft

- IC1: Executes designs to spec, follows established patterns
- IC2: Produces complete, polished work; uses the system competently
- IC3: Drives craft quality on owned projects; identifies inconsistencies
- IC4: Elevates craft beyond their projects; sets standards for the team
- IC5: Defines what good looks like at the org level

### 2. Problem framing

- IC1: Solves problems as defined
- IC2: Clarifies fuzzy briefs through good questions
- IC3: Reframes problems when they're miscast
- IC4: Identifies the right problem before others see it
- IC5: Shapes the problem space the org is working in

### 3. Collaboration

- IC1: Works well with assigned partners
- IC2: Initiates cross-functional conversations
- IC3: Navigates ambiguity and disagreement productively
- IC4: Builds trust and alignment across multiple stakeholders
- IC5: Builds the collaboration norms others follow

### 4. Strategic thinking

- IC1: Understands their project's goals
- IC2: Connects feature work to product strategy
- IC3: Anticipates downstream implications of design choices
- IC4: Influences product strategy through design framing
- IC5: Shapes the design strategy that informs product strategy

### 5. Communication

- IC1: Clear in 1:1 settings, finds presenting hard
- IC2: Can present own work confidently
- IC3: Tells the story of why a design matters
- IC4: Communicates effectively up to senior leadership
- IC5: Sets narrative for the design organization

### 6. Leadership / Mentorship (IC3+)

- IC3: Helps junior designers; gives useful critique
- IC4: Mentors juniors and mid-level designers; shapes hiring
- IC5: Develops design leaders; shapes the function

## Expectations behaviorally (not just outcomes)

For each level, list 3-5 behavioral signals.

### Senior (IC3)
- Drives projects without requiring direction from me
- Critiques work in ways that improve it (not just identifies issues)
- Identifies risks before they become blockers
- Mentors juniors informally
- Pushes back on bad briefs constructively

### Staff (IC4)
- Owns design for a product area, not just a project
- Partners with PM/Eng leadership on roadmap
- Shapes hiring and contributes to leveling decisions
- Mentors seniors
- Sets craft standards for the team

### Principal (IC5)
- Operates on org-wide problems
- Shapes design org strategy
- Has external presence (writing, speaking, recruiting)
- Builds design leaders

## Promotion criteria

Promotion to level N requires:
1. **Operating at level N for 6+ months consistently** (not occasionally)
2. **Evidence across capability dimensions** (not just craft)
3. **Endorsement from cross-functional partners** at level N
4. **Org headroom and need** for someone operating at level N

Operating at level N occasionally is "stretch", not "ready."

## Notes for managers using this ladder

- Update annually
- Calibrate with peer managers — your IC3 should look like their IC3
- Reference language in 1:1s and career conversations
- Don't promote on craft alone — multi-dimensional growth required
- Track behavioral signals as much as outcomes
